Tree-Based Models

Decision Trees & Random Forests

A doctor playing "20 questions." A jury reaching a verdict. That's the soul of tree-based machine learning — and the reason these models dominate spreadsheet-style data everywhere.

Manager Engineer Social Scientist Curious Beginner
1 The Idea

A doctor's checklist, automated

Every decision tree is a sequence of yes/no questions. The model learns which questions to ask first, in what order, to separate outcomes most cleanly.

"Should I bring an umbrella?"

Imagine your morning routine:

Is it cloudy? → If yes, did it rain yesterday? → If yes, bring umbrella.

That's literally a decision tree. The clever part is the model figuring out — from past mornings — that "cloudy" is the most informative first question, not "what day of the week is it?"

Why trees are loved: You can read them. Show a tree to a doctor, a banker, a regulator — they immediately understand the logic.

3 Where Trees Go Wrong

Deeper isn't always smarter

A tree that's allowed to grow forever will memorise the training data perfectly — and fail on anything new. This is overfitting, the cardinal sin of machine learning.

4 Strength in Numbers

Why a forest beats a single tree

One opinionated tree can be wrong. Random Forest trains many slightly different trees on different slices of data, and takes a vote. The errors cancel out — like a jury, not a dictator.

Why this works: Each tree saw only ~63% of the data (different random sample) and only some features at each split. They make different mistakes. Averaging away their errors is what statisticians call variance reduction.

⚠️ Honest caveat: Feature importance tells you what the model used — not what causes churn in reality. A high-importance feature can still be a proxy for something else (e.g., zip code → income). Treat it as a strong hint, not a causal proof.

7 Check Yourself

Three quick questions

1Your single decision tree scores 99% on training data but 64% on test data. What's happening?
Correct: The huge train/test gap is the signature of overfitting. Fixes: cap max_depth, require more samples per split, or move to Random Forest.
2Why is a Random Forest usually better than a single tree?
Correct: This is "variance reduction" — the wisdom-of-crowds effect. Each tree alone is noisy; the average is steady.
3The model lists "zip code" as the most important feature for predicting loan default. What should a responsible analyst do?
Correct: Feature importance shows what the model used — not whether using it is ethical or causal. Always interrogate top features for proxy variables and fairness implications.

What to carry away

1
Decision trees are sequences of yes/no questions that anyone can read and follow.
2
A tree picks each split by asking "which question makes the cleanest groups?"
3
Overfitting happens when a tree grows so deep it memorises training quirks.
4
Random Forest = many trees on slightly different data, voting together.
5
Variance reduction is the magic: errors cancel when trees disagree differently.
6
Feature importance shows what the model used — not necessarily what truly causes the outcome.

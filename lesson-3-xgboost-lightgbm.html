Gradient Boosting

XGBoost & LightGBM

Why do data scientists almost always reach for these two libraries first when handed a spreadsheet? Because boosting — teaching trees to fix each other's mistakes — has quietly won most tabular ML competitions for a decade.

Manager Engineer Social Scientist Curious Beginner
1 The Idea

A student improving on each homework

Random forest sends 100 students into separate rooms and averages their answers. Boosting trains one student at a time — and each one studies only the questions the previous students got wrong.

The tutor's secret

Imagine teaching a beginner to predict tomorrow's temperature. First tree simply says: "30°C — same as today." Crude, but a starting point.

Now we look at where it was wrong — maybe it overshoots on cloudy days. Second tree doesn't try to predict temperature from scratch. It only learns the correction: "subtract 3 if cloudy."

Third tree: "subtract another 1 if there was wind at 5pm." And so on. After 500 tiny corrections, the combined model is razor-sharp.

The trick: Each tree is shallow, weak, and slightly off — but together they form a precision instrument. This is gradient boosting, and XGBoost & LightGBM are its two most refined implementations.

How we trust a single number

If we tune hyperparameters on one test split, we risk fitting to its quirks. K-fold CV rotates the test set K times — each row gets to be "the test" exactly once.

5 Playground D — XGBoost vs LightGBM

Two ways to grow the same tree

Same boosting idea, different tree-growth strategy. The result: subtle but real trade-offs in speed, accuracy, and how easily each one overfits.

D Level-wise vs leaf-wise
click "grow"

XGBoost Level-wise

Grows the tree one level at a time, splitting all leaves on a level before moving deeper. Balanced and predictable.

LightGBM Leaf-wise

Grows the tree by splitting the single most "profitable" leaf at each step. Faster to reduce error, but skewed and overfit-prone on small data.

Step 0: Both trees start as a single root node. Click "Grow next step" to see how they diverge.
When to prefer XGBoost
Small/medium datasets, when you want stable & reproducible behaviour, when the data is noisy and overfitting is a concern.
When to prefer LightGBM
Large datasets (millions of rows), many categorical features, when training speed matters. Set num_leaves conservatively.
6 Case Study

Credit default at a fintech lender

Real-world scenario

"Will this borrower repay?"

A fintech has 400,000 historical loans, each labelled "repaid" or "defaulted." Features include income, age, employment length, debt-to-income ratio, prior late payments, loan amount, region, and 18 others. The team trains an XGBoost model. Test AUC: 0.84. Three people look at the same number.

Risk Manager
"AUC 0.84 means if I pick a random defaulter and a random repayer, the model ranks them correctly 84% of the time. That lets me set a cutoff where I approve the top 60% of applicants and reject only 8% of repayers by mistake. Better than our current logistic baseline at 0.78."
Data Scientist
"I tuned max_depth=6, η=0.05, n_estimators=800 using 5-fold CV. Used early stopping — training halted at round 642 because validation error stopped improving. Saved hours and prevented overfit."
Compliance Officer
"Auditors will ask why a specific person was denied. XGBoost's 800 trees aren't human-readable. We must pair this with SHAP (Lesson 5) and prove the model isn't using protected attributes — region could secretly encode caste or religion. Document, don't just deploy."
7 Quiz

Quick check

1 Your training error keeps falling, but your validation error stopped improving after round 200 and is now slowly rising. What's happening?
A classic boosting overfit signature: train ↓, validation ↑. The fix is early stopping — most libraries do this automatically when you pass an eval set. Reducing max_depth also helps by making each tree weaker.
2 You halve the learning rate from 0.1 to 0.05. To keep the same model capacity, what else should change?
Each tree now contributes half as much, so you need roughly twice as many to cover the same ground. This learning-rate/n-estimators trade-off is one of the most reliable rules of thumb in boosting — smaller η plus more rounds usually generalises better.
3 Your dataset is 8 million rows with 200 features, half of which are high-cardinality categoricals (city, sub-district). Which library is the more natural first pick?
LightGBM was engineered for large data with many categoricals — it uses histogram-based splits and a special algorithm (GOSS) to subsample. XGBoost is excellent too, but on this scale LightGBM typically trains 3-10× faster with comparable accuracy. Watch for overfit on smaller validation slices.
8 Takeaways

What to carry forward

The six things to remember

1Boosting = sequential correction. Each tree fixes the leftover errors of the previous trees, not the original target.
2Small η + many rounds almost always beats large η + few rounds. Be patient and pair it with early stopping.
3K-fold CV is how you trust a hyperparameter choice. One test split can lie; five test splits rarely all do.
4XGBoost vs LightGBM is mostly about scale and growth strategy. Try both — they take 30 minutes to swap in.
5For tabular data, these libraries still beat deep learning in most real-world benchmarks. They are the default, not the fallback.
6Accuracy isn't enough. Once you have a 0.84 AUC, the next question is why — that's what Lesson 5 (SHAP) answers.

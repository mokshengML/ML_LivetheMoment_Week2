Lesson 4 of 5
Metrics that matter

Model Evaluation

Accuracy is a comforting number — and often a deeply misleading one. This lesson is about the four small tools that let you actually understand whether a classifier is good: the confusion matrix, ROC, precision-recall, and F1.

Manager Engineer Social Scientist Curious Beginner
1 The Idea

"99% accurate" can be terrible

If 99 out of 100 emails in your inbox are real and 1 is spam, a model that says "real" to everything achieves 99% accuracy — while catching zero spam. Accuracy alone hides everything that matters.

The security guard's dilemma

A guard checks IDs at a stadium gate. Most people are genuine fans; a tiny fraction carry fake tickets.

Be too strict — you turn away real fans (wrongly rejected). Be too lax — fakes get through. The guard's behaviour at the cutoff is the model's threshold.

Every classifier outputs a probability score (0 to 1). We pick a threshold — say 0.5 — above which we say "positive." That single number controls a brutal trade-off. Lesson 4 is about visualising it.

The vocabulary: True positive (caught a fake correctly), False positive (turned away a real fan), True negative (waved a real fan through), False negative (let a fake through).

Definitions (memorise these):
Precision = TP / (TP + FP) — of the patients we flagged, what fraction are actually sick?
Recall (also called sensitivity) = TP / (TP + FN) — of the actually sick, what fraction did we catch?
F1 = 2·P·R / (P+R) — the harmonic mean. Punishes you if either precision or recall is very low.

What does the model look like at every threshold at once?

The ROC curve traces the model's true-positive rate vs false-positive rate as we sweep the threshold from 1.0 down to 0.0. The area under it (AUC) is a threshold-free summary of model quality.

5 Playground D — Pick the right metric

Three scenarios, three different metrics

Click each card. The choice depends on what kind of mistake you can least afford to make.

🏥
Cancer screening
A blood-test model flags patients for follow-up biopsy. Missing a sick patient is catastrophic; a false alarm causes anxiety and an extra appointment.
📧
Email spam filter
Marks emails as spam. Missing a spam email is mildly annoying; marking your boss's email as spam can cost you a job.
💳
Credit-card fraud
Each day, 5 out of 100,000 transactions are fraud. We must catch many of them — but a freeze on legitimate transactions is a customer-service disaster.

🏥 Cancer screening — prioritise recall

The asymmetry is enormous. A missed cancer (false negative) can mean a delayed diagnosis and a far worse outcome. A false positive means an extra biopsy — uncomfortable but not catastrophic.

Use: Recall (sensitivity). Tune the threshold down until recall ≥ 0.95. Accept the false-positive cost. Also report the full ROC curve so clinicians can make their own trade-off.
Avoid: Plain accuracy. If only 1% of patients are sick, a model that says "healthy" to everyone is 99% accurate and 100% useless.

📧 Spam filter — prioritise precision

Here the asymmetry flips. A spam email that slips through (false negative) takes one second to delete. A legitimate email lost to the spam folder (false positive) can ruin a deal or destroy trust in the product.

Use: Precision. Tune the threshold up until precision is very high (≥0.99). Recall will drop, but that's acceptable — users would rather see some spam than miss real mail.
Avoid: Optimising recall here. Quarantining 99% of spam at the cost of 1% of real mail is unacceptable in a personal inbox.

💳 Fraud detection — use F1 or PR-AUC, and report both

Fraud is highly imbalanced (≈0.005% of transactions). Both error types are painful: missed fraud costs the bank money; falsely freezing a card costs customer goodwill. We need balance, not an extreme.

Use: F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall) and the area under the PR curve. Both stay honest when the positive class is rare. Set business-level cost weights if known.
Avoid: ROC-AUC as the only metric. With 99.995% negatives, ROC can look great while precision is still 5%.

6 Case Study

"Our model is 92% accurate" — three reactions

Boardroom moment

The hospital-readmission model

A health system builds an ML model that predicts whether a discharged patient will be re-admitted within 30 days. Out of 10,000 historical patients, 800 actually were re-admitted (8% positive rate). The model achieves 92% accuracy. The team brings it to leadership.

CEO
"92% sounds great. Ship it." The trap: a model that says "no readmission" to everyone scores 92% too — it would catch zero of the 800 patients we actually need to help. We need to know recall on the positive class, not accuracy.
Clinical Director
"Show me the confusion matrix." The right question. The team reports TP=520, FN=280, FP=520, TN=8680. Recall = 65%. Precision = 50%. We catch two-thirds of at-risk patients, and half our flags are false alarms — a meaningful but imperfect tool.
Operations Lead
"What's the cost of each flag?" The decisive question. A flag triggers a 20-minute nurse call. If 520 false alarms cost ₹260,000 in nurse time, but 520 prevented readmissions save ₹26 lakh in hospital costs — the model pays for itself 10× over.

No metric stands alone. The right number is the one tied to a business decision and a real cost.

7 Quiz

Quick check

1 A model predicting rare bird sightings reports AUC = 0.92. Only 0.3% of the test set are actual sightings. Should you celebrate?
With 0.3% positives, ROC can look great while precision is still 5–10%. The PR curve and average precision (AP) reflect what actually happens at deployment thresholds. For imbalanced data, always look at PR alongside ROC.
2 You raise the classification threshold from 0.5 to 0.7. Which usually happens?
A higher threshold makes us more conservative — we only call something "positive" when very confident. That gives cleaner positive predictions (higher precision) but we miss more (lower recall). It's a fundamental trade-off — pick whichever side fits the cost of your mistakes.
3 A teammate proposes optimising the model for F1 in a cancer-screening setting. Your reaction?
F1 is the harmonic mean — it assumes precision and recall matter equally. They almost never do. In cancer screening, the cost of a false negative dwarfs the cost of a false positive, so recall-weighted F2 or just plain recall at a fixed precision floor is far more appropriate.
8 Takeaways

What to carry forward

The six things to remember

1Accuracy is the first metric to distrust. Especially on imbalanced data, it can be very high and very useless.
2The confusion matrix is the source of truth. Every other metric is just a way of summarising those four numbers.
3Threshold ≠ model. One trained model produces a different confusion matrix at every threshold. Plot them all with ROC or PR.
4Use ROC when classes are roughly balanced — it's threshold-free and intuitive.
5Use PR (and average precision) when the positive class is rare — fraud, disease screening, anomaly detection.
6F1 is convenient, not always right. Always ask: which error costs more? The answer chooses your metric.

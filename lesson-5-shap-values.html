Explaining the black box

SHAP Values

Powerful models — XGBoost, random forests, neural networks — are accurate but opaque. SHAP is the tool that lets you ask "why did the model predict this, for this person?" — and get an answer that's mathematically rigorous, not just a hunch.

Manager Engineer Social Scientist Curious Beginner
1 The Idea

Sharing credit for the win

Imagine a cricket team wins a match and the captain has ₹100,000 in prize money to split. Some players bowled brilliantly, some fielded well, some never touched the ball. How do you split it fairly? That exact question — from game theory — is what SHAP solves for model predictions.

The team-bonus analogy

A model's prediction for a single person is the "win." The features (income, credit score, age…) are the "players." SHAP asks: how much did each feature contribute to moving this prediction away from the average?

The answer comes from a beautifully simple thought experiment. Imagine the player joining the team in every possible order. Sometimes the team is already strong — their contribution is small. Sometimes the team is weak — the same player is the difference-maker. Average across all orderings, and you get a fair share. That fair share is the Shapley value, invented by Lloyd Shapley in 1953 and adapted to ML as SHAP in 2017.

The result has one magical property: the SHAP values for one row sum to exactly the prediction minus the baseline. Nothing is fudged. No information leaks. The accounting always balances.

The promise: for any single prediction, SHAP gives you a complete, additive breakdown — "the model said 23% because of X, Y, Z." You can show this to a regulator, a customer, or a colleague and defend every number.

4 Playground C

From many stories to one ranking

Zoom out. Compute SHAP for every row in the dataset, then average the absolute values per feature. The result is global feature importance — and unlike older importance metrics, it's grounded in the same per-prediction math, so the global and local views always agree.

C Global feature importance
Mean |SHAP| across 2,000 applicants
Average impact on prediction
Bars show mean |SHAP|: average size of each feature's push, ignoring direction. A small bar can still flip a single prediction — it just doesn't do so on average.
Sample size
Stable ranking. With enough rows, the order barely changes — credit score and income are clearly the dominant drivers, with debt ratio close behind. Age contributes the least on average, but as Maya's case showed, it can matter for individuals.
Why mean |SHAP| beats traditional "importance": tree-based importance (gain, split count) reflects how the model was built. SHAP reflects how the model behaves on real data. Two models that score identically can have very different gain rankings — but they will have the same SHAP profile if they truly make the same predictions.
5 Playground D

Where the magic hides — interactions

A dependence plot puts a feature's value on the x-axis and its SHAP value on the y-axis. Each dot is one applicant. The shape reveals whether the relationship is linear, has a threshold, or — most interestingly — depends on a second feature.

D SHAP dependence — Income
Colour reveals interaction with credit score
Credit score: low mid high
Two patterns at once. First, income's effect is non-linear: SHAP rises steeply up to about ₹15L, then plateaus — extra income beyond that barely helps. Second, the colour gradient shows that the same income contributes differently for applicants with different credit scores. That's an interaction — a pattern that no single bar chart can show.
What you can do with this: set policy thresholds (e.g. "income above ₹15L is treated equally"), spot non-linearities a linear model would miss, or detect interactions you'd otherwise need an explicit cross-feature for.
A caution. If two features are highly correlated (e.g. income and savings balance), SHAP will split the credit between them somewhat arbitrarily. The sum of their SHAPs is still right, but reading either one in isolation can mislead.
6 Case Study

"Why was my loan rejected?"

Three people, one decision, three different reasons to care about the answer.

FinTech · Loan Origination · Adverse-action explanations

Maya gets the email

A mid-sized lender uses an XGBoost model that scores loan applications. Maya, a 26-year-old marketing executive, applies for ₹18L. The model returns 0.23 — below the bank's 0.50 cutoff. The system auto-rejects her.

In many regulated jurisdictions, the bank is legally required to send Maya a letter explaining why — an "adverse action notice." Saying "the model said no" is not allowed. Before SHAP, banks would either fall back to a simpler (less accurate) model or hand-craft generic explanations. Now they generate the force plot from Section 2, translate it into plain language, and ship it.

What follows is the same SHAP output read by three different stakeholders, each with their own use for it.

Maya (the applicant)
She gets a letter that says: "Your application was declined primarily because your income (₹5L) and your employment tenure (1 year) fell below typical thresholds for this loan amount." Crucially, it tells her what to do: wait two years for tenure, or apply for a smaller loan. SHAP turned an opaque "no" into actionable feedback.
Loan Officer
She sees the force plot directly. She can audit whether the rejection follows policy ("we don't lend to first-year employees over ₹15L") and decide whether to manually escalate Maya's case. Without SHAP she'd have only the score — and zero context.
Compliance & Fairness Officer
Aggregating SHAP across thousands of applications, she checks that protected attributes (gender, religion) aren't driving rejections — directly or via proxies like name or pincode. SHAP is the audit trail that makes regulated ML deployment defensible.
7 Check yourself

Three questions

Click an answer to reveal whether it's right and why.

1 For a single applicant, you add up all the SHAP values across features. What does the sum equal?
Yes. The defining mathematical guarantee of SHAP is local accuracy: sum of SHAPs + baseline = the exact prediction. This is what makes SHAP usable for audit and regulation — the explanation never "loses" or "invents" probability mass.
2 Income has the largest global SHAP importance. For Anjali, income's SHAP value is +0.05. What can you conclude?
Exactly. Global importance is an average. A feature that's strong on average can be a minor factor for any individual — and Anjali's high debt ratio (−0.18) is what actually drives her rejection. This is precisely why local explanations matter: averages obscure stories.
3 A bank shows SHAP plots in its adverse-action letters and concludes the system is fair. What's still missing?
Right. SHAP is a description of the model, not a verdict on its ethics. If the model learned to reject applicants from certain pincodes — and pincode correlates with caste or religion — SHAP will show "pincode contributed −0.12" without flagging the protected-attribute problem. Interpretability is necessary for fairness; it isn't sufficient.
8 Recap

The six things to remember

Pin these to your wall

1SHAP is local first. It explains one prediction at a time, not the model as a whole. That's its superpower — and what generic feature-importance can't do.
2The accounting always balances. Sum of SHAP values + baseline = prediction. This guarantee comes from game theory and makes SHAP defensible in court, in regulation, and in adverse-action letters.
3Global importance is mean |SHAP|. Average the absolute SHAP value per feature across many rows. The result is grounded in the same math as the local view — they always agree.
4Dependence plots reveal what bars hide. Non-linearities, thresholds, and feature interactions show up as patterns in the dependence scatter — invisible in a single bar chart.
5Beware correlated features. SHAP will split credit between two highly correlated features somewhat arbitrarily. Read pairs of related features together, not in isolation.
6SHAP is not causality, and not fairness. It tells you what the model is doing. Whether the model should be doing that is a separate, human conversation that SHAP enables but does not resolve.
